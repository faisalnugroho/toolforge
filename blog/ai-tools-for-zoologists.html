How AI is changing zoology

The biggest impact is in data processing. A typical camera trap study produces 100,000+ images. Ten years ago, a researcher would spend months manually reviewing them. MegaDetector processes them in hours, automatically filtering out empty frames and flagging animal detections for species classification.

Acoustic monitoring has been similarly transformed. Tools like BirdNET can identify species from audio recordings with 90%+ accuracy, enabling continuous biodiversity monitoring without human observers. This is especially valuable for nocturnal and cryptic species.

Individual identification — recognizing specific animals by their unique markings — used to be entirely manual. Wildbook's AI can identify individual whale sharks from spot patterns, elephants from ear shapes, and big cats from stripe/spot patterns. This enables population estimates and movement tracking without physical tagging.

The workflow

For a field zoologist, the AI stack looks like this: Deploy camera traps and acoustic recorders in the field. Process camera trap images with MegaDetector to find animals, then use iNaturalist's API or custom classifiers for species ID. Run audio through BirdNET for avian biodiversity surveys. Use Wildbook for individual animal tracking. Analyze habitat change with Google Earth Engine. Use Perplexity for literature review and Claude for writing papers and grant applications.

The cost? Remarkably low. Most field research tools (iNaturalist, BirdNET, MegaDetector, Wildbook) are free and open-source. A Pro subscription to Perplexity ($20/mo) and Claude ($20/mo) rounds out the stack. Total: $40/month for a research-grade AI toolkit.

What to skip

Don't use general-purpose image generators for creating illustrations of species — they produce anatomically incorrect results. Don't rely on ChatGPT for species identification from photos — it's not trained on taxonomic data and will hallucinate. And skip commercial "wildlife AI" platforms unless you specifically need their enterprise features — the open-source tools are often better and always free.
