How AI is changing cartography

Cartography has always been data-intensive, but AI is transforming how that data gets processed. Three areas stand out:

1. Automated feature extraction. AI models can now automatically extract roads, buildings, water bodies, and land cover from satellite imagery — tasks that used to take weeks of manual digitization. Google Earth Engine and ArcGIS AI both offer pre-trained models for common extraction tasks, with the ability to fine-tune on custom datasets.

2. Change detection. AI can compare satellite images from different time periods and automatically identify what changed — new construction, deforestation, urban sprawl. This used to require painstaking visual comparison. Now it's a few API calls.

3. Code generation for GIS. LLMs like ChatGPT and Claude are surprisingly good at writing Python scripts for QGIS, GDAL, and GeoPandas. Need to reproject a shapefile, calculate zonal statistics, or generate a choropleth? Describe it in English and get working code.

The workflow

A modern cartographer's AI stack: Use Google Earth Engine for satellite imagery processing and land cover classification. Use ArcGIS AI or open-source alternatives (Deepness plugin for QGIS) for feature extraction. Build custom interactive maps with Mapbox. Use ChatGPT to generate Python/JavaScript code for data processing and visualization. Use Claude for writing documentation and methodology sections. Use Perplexity to find datasets and research mapping standards.

Cost: Google Earth Engine is free for research. ArcGIS starts at ~$100/month for the AI features. ChatGPT Plus and Claude Pro are $20/month each. Total: about $140/month for a professional cartography AI stack.

What to skip

Don't use AI image generators for creating map visuals — they can't handle accurate geography and will produce fictional coastlines and place names. Don't rely on LLMs for coordinate calculations without verification — they make arithmetic errors. And skip the "AI map makers" that claim to generate maps from text prompts — they're not suitable for professional cartographic work.
